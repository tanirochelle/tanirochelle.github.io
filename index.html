August 2026 to present · NR 450 course project · Ongoing

Atmospheric flooding analysis of San Diego

San Diego flooded on January 22, 2024. My partner and I are testing how well terrain explains where the water went, and what the city could do next.

My project partner and I used Google Earth Engine to pull CHIRPS precipitation, Sentinel-1 radar (SAR) imagery and JRC surface water data, and to analyze precipitation from the atmospheric floods of 2024. We are beginning the terrain analysis with a Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) and plan to add a Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND) analysis, then compare both against the floods themselves.

The severity of the flooding was underreported, so we also compiled a heat map of Get It Done calls, the city’s non-emergency reports, to show where residents were asking for help.

From there we will evaluate the mitigation and adaptation techniques used in Norfolk, Virginia, as a case study for what can be proposed in San Diego to prevent flooding that exceeds sea level rise.

Progress, October 2026

Precipitation, SAR and surface water data are pulled, and the Get It Done heat map and first TWI map are drafted. The HAND analysis, the comparison against observed flooding and the Norfolk case study come next.

Project workflow

  1. Data acquisition

    Identify vector and raster sources, pull datasets through Google Earth Engine and collect Get It Done non-emergency calls.

  2. Verification and cleaning

    Reproject layers, check for missing data, reclassify rasters, clip to the city boundary and geocode the Get It Done data.

  3. Elevation and congestion maps

    Derive slope and aspect from digital elevation models, and map traffic congestion density around the entry and exit points of the main bases.

  4. Major flood event modeling

    Find the top precipitation events in CHIRPS, extract post-storm surface water from Sentinel-1 SAR, mask permanent water with the JRC baseline and compare against FEMA and First Street flood hazard layers.

  5. Hydrological modeling

    Run TWI and HAND from the elevation models, calculate flow accumulation, and validate modeled susceptibility against the SAR observations.

  6. Spatial analysis

    Overlay flood extent on roads and critical infrastructure, then measure flooded area, impacted road miles and affected population.

  7. Adaptation and mitigation strategies

    Assess sites by slope, soil permeability and land use, and prioritize where interventions would matter most.

  8. Interactive visualization

    Build web maps with layer toggles and legend controls, exported as standalone pages.

  9. Research paper

    Compile static maps, method figures and summary tables into a reproducible manuscript.

May 2026 to present · Undergraduate research · Ongoing

Comprehensive review of PM2.5 prediction models

Several research groups publish national maps of fine particulate pollution. I mapped where they disagree.

PM2.5 is fine inhalable particulate matter, 2.5 micrometers and smaller, and it poses a risk for a wide range of health issues. Health and policy research depends on models that predict its concentration everywhere, including the places between ground monitors.

Working with Associate Professor Kayleigh Keller, I compared the available datasets: ACAG, GHAP and its U.S. counterpart USHAP, SEDAC, and CACES. They arrive as a mix of raster and vector data on different grids. I built a master grid, matched each model to its coordinate reference system and projection, and resampled with bilinear interpolation so the models could be differenced cell by cell. All of the work is in R.

Key findings

  • No two independent datasets are aligned.
  • GHAP predicts significantly higher concentrations than the others.
  • ACAG’s regional model is a crop of its global model, and the two are identical.
  • The western United States is the most uncertain region.

Where it is going

This began as a paid summer position, and I was asked to continue with the professor into the fall. We have since evaluated the models against EPA sensors and extended the comparison to NO2 and O3. I am now working toward a comprehensive review of all exposome models, with an urban and rural comparison.

Suitable nuclear power plant sites in Utah

Where are the prime sites to build a nuclear power plant in Utah?

Energy demand is rising, pushed in part by AI data centers, and Utah has expressed interest in building a nuclear plant. For the final project of my first GIS course, I asked where in the state one could go.

I took five siting constraints from the federal Reactor Site Criteria (10 CFR Part 100) and turned each one into a map layer. I built the analysis in ArcGIS Pro ModelBuilder so that every step can be rerun: buffers around highways, railroads, fault lines and water, a union of the excluded areas, an erase against the state boundary, and a merge with the layers for suitable elevation and low population.

The result is a short list of candidate areas in south-central and southeastern Utah, delivered as a final site map and a 3D scene.
